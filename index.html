Introduction

Hammer a handful of nails into a board, stretch a rubber band around all of them and let it go. The band snaps onto the outermost nails and forms a convex polygon. That polygon is the convex hull of the nails.

In geometric terms, the convex hull $CH(S)$ of a finite set $S$ of points in the plane is the smallest convex set that contains $S$. Its boundary is a convex polygon whose corners are points of $S$, and computing $CH(S)$ means finding those corners, in order. Notice how, in the board above, most nails never touch the band: deciding quickly which points can be ignored is exactly what makes a hull algorithm fast.

The convex hull is one of the oldest problems in computational geometry and a building block for many others, from collision detection to measuring the width of a shape. There are several classic ways to compute it [11, 12]. This site is about one of the most widely used in practice: Quickhull, proposed independently by Eddy [6] and Bykat [4] in the late 1970s.

Quickhull in four moves

Quickhull borrows the strategy of quicksort: split the problem around a pivot, throw away whatever is already settled, and repeat on the pieces. Here the pivot is a point that is guaranteed to be on the hull: the one farthest from a dividing line.

  1. Split

    The points with the smallest and the largest $x$, $a$ and $b$, are always on the hull. The line $ab$ cuts the cloud into two halves that can be solved separately.

  2. Find the farthest point

    On one side, take the point $c$ farthest from the line $ab$. Nothing lies beyond the parallel line through $c$, so $c$ is on the hull too.

  3. Discard

    Every point inside the triangle $acb$ is enclosed by three hull points, so it can never be a corner. It is discarded without a second look.

  4. Repeat

    Do the same with the points outside $ac$ and outside $cb$, and on the other half. When a segment has no points left outside, it is an edge of the hull.

Why it works

The farthest point is always a corner

Draw the line through $c$ parallel to $ab$. Since no point of the subproblem is farther from $ab$ than $c$, the whole set lies on one side of that line, which touches it only at $c$. A point with a line like that is a vertex of $CH(S)$. (If several points tie, they lie on that line and only its endpoints are corners, so one of them is chosen.)

The triangle is safe to throw away

The points $a$, $b$ and $c$ are on the hull, and $CH(S)$ is convex, so the whole triangle $acb$ is inside it. A point strictly inside the triangle can be written as a combination of the three corners, so it can never be a corner itself.

One formula does all the geometry

Every decision Quickhull makes, which side of a line a point is on and how far away it is, comes from the signed area of a triangle $pqr$:

$$\operatorname{area}(p,q,r) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\begin{vmatrix} q_x - p_x & r_x - p_x \\ q_y - p_y & r_y - p_y \end{vmatrix}$$

It is positive when $r$ is to the left of the directed line $p \to q$, negative when it is to the right, and zero when the three points are collinear. For a fixed segment $pq$, the distance from $r$ to the line is $2\,|\operatorname{area}(p,q,r)| / |pq|$, so the farthest point is simply the one with the largest $|\operatorname{area}|$: no square roots, no divisions.

The algorithm

Written with the same notation as the lecture notes [14]. $S$ always holds the points to the right of the directed segment, that is, outside the part of the hull built so far.

FUNCTION Quickhull(a, b, S)            // S: points to the right of a→b
  if S is empty: return [ ]          // ab is an edge of CH(S)
  c ← point of S farthest from line ab
  A ← points of S to the right of a→c
  B ← points of S to the right of c→b
                                      // the rest lie inside triangle acb
  return Quickhull(a, c, A) + [c] + Quickhull(c, b, B)

QUICKHULL(S)
  a, b ← points of S with minimum and maximum x
  return [a] + Quickhull(a, b, right of a→b)
       + [b] + Quickhull(b, a, right of b→a)

The result is the list of corners of $CH(S)$ in counter-clockwise order. The SageMath version used by the applet follows this structure line by line, with one change explained in the discussion: an explicit stack instead of recursion.

How fast is it?

Each call scans its points once, $O(n)$, and then solves two smaller problems: $T(n) = T(|A|) + T(|B|) + O(n)$. How small $A$ and $B$ are decides everything.

CaseWhat happensRecurrenceCost
BestA single split leaves nothing outside the triangles$T(n) = O(n)$$O(n)$
TypicalSplits are roughly balanced and most points are discarded early$T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + O(n)$$O(n \log n)$
WorstEvery split leaves one side empty and removes only one point$T(n) = T(n-1) + O(n)$$O(n^2)$

All points on the hull is not enough for the worst case

If every point is a corner, no point can be discarded, but Quickhull can still be fast: points spread evenly around a circle are split right down the middle every time, and the cost stays $O(n \log n)$. The worst case also needs unbalanced splits. The applet builds one by placing the points on the parabola $y = x^2$ at $x = 1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \dots$, where the farthest point from each chord is always the next one in the list. Try it in the benchmark and watch the exponent jump to 2.

Discussion and real-world applications

Quickhull among its relatives

Quickhull is one of several classic ways to build a convex hull. Here $n$ is the number of points and $h$ the number of corners of the hull.

AlgorithmIdeaTime
Jarvis march [9]Wraps the set like gift paper, one hull edge at a time$O(nh)$
Graham scan [8]Sorts the points by angle, then removes the concave turns with a stack$O(n \log n)$
Divide and conquerSplits by $x$, solves both halves and merges them with two tangents$O(n \log n)$
IncrementalAdds the points one by one in $x$ order and repairs the hull$O(n \log n)$
Quickhull [6, 4]Splits at the farthest point and discards a whole triangle$O(n \log n)$ typical, $O(n^2)$ worst
Chan [5]Combines Graham and Jarvis on small groups$O(n \log h)$, optimal

On paper, Quickhull looks like the weakest of the list because of its quadratic worst case. In practice it is often the fastest: it needs no sorting, and on typical data the first few triangles swallow most of the points, so the work that remains is tiny. It shines when the hull has few corners compared with the number of points, which is the usual situation with real measurements.

What the benchmark shows

These are the results we obtained with the applet on SageMathCell. Times change with the load of the server, but the trends repeat every time.

  • Random clouds: with 10,000 points uniform in a square, Quickhull is about 2 times faster than Graham. Both have an empirical exponent of about 1.04: at these sizes, $n \log n$ looks almost linear.
  • Worst case: with 300 points on the parabola, Quickhull is about 22 times slower than Graham and its exponent rises to 1.9, close to the 2 predicted by $T(n) = T(n-1) + O(n)$.
  • Every point on the hull, but balanced: on a circle, all points are corners and Quickhull is still $O(n \log n)$. In our tests it was around 3 times slower than Graham there, because it does one subproblem per edge of the hull.
  • The quadrilateral filter discards between 30% and 55% of a random cloud before Quickhull even starts, but it does not make our Python code faster: it costs up to four orientation tests per point, while the first split of Quickhull only needs one or two. On the worst-case parabola it discards nothing at all, because the point with the smallest $x$ is also the one with the smallest $y$. Akl and Toussaint [1] proposed it as a cheap first pass, and it pays off most for algorithms that must handle every point anyway, such as Graham's sort.

Implementation notes

  • A stack instead of recursion. In the worst case the recursion would go $n$ levels deep, and Python stops at about 1,000 levels. Keeping the pending subproblems on an explicit stack gives exactly the same order of work without that limit.
  • Exact ties. When several points are equally far from $pq$ they lie on a line parallel to it, and only the two ends of that segment are corners. The code picks one of the ends, so points lying on an edge are never reported as corners. We checked the result against an independent hull algorithm on about 36,000 clouds, including repeated points and many collinear ones.
  • Plain numbers. Points are stored as pairs of floating-point numbers. SageMath vectors read more naturally, but they create a new object in every operation, which makes the inner loop several times slower.
  • Computing and drawing are separate. SageMath computes the hull and records every step once. Your browser then draws those steps, which is why the player can go back and forth without asking the server again.

Beyond the plane

Quickhull works in any dimension. In 3D, the dividing line becomes a face, the farthest point is the one farthest from that face, and the discarded region is a pyramid. Barber, Dobkin and Huhdanpaa [2] turned this idea into Qhull, a library that computes hulls, Delaunay triangulations and Voronoi diagrams in several dimensions. Qhull is the engine behind the convex hull functions of SciPy, MATLAB, Octave and R [15]. If you have ever called scipy.spatial.ConvexHull, you have run Quickhull.

Where convex hulls show up

Collision detection
Games and robots wrap complex objects in convex shapes, because deciding whether two convex sets touch is fast. Algorithms such as GJK [7] measure the distance between convex objects in real time.
Image analysis
OpenCV computes the hull of a shape's outline and its convexity defects, the gaps between the outline and its hull. The deepest gaps of a hand outline, for example, sit between the fingers, which is a classic way of counting them [15].
Ecology
The minimum convex polygon is the convex hull of all the places where an animal has been observed. Since Mohr [10] it has been a standard first estimate of an animal's home range, although it tends to overestimate it.
Measuring shapes
The two farthest points of a cloud, its diameter, are always corners of the hull, and so are the points that define its width. Once the hull is known, the rotating calipers [13] find both in linear time instead of checking every pair of points.
Statistics
Removing the hull of a data set again and again, like peeling an onion, orders multivariate data from the outside in [3]. The outer layers flag possible outliers and the innermost one gives a robust idea of the centre.
